# Paper

Research or a lab project written up: a question, a method, results and their limits. A paper has one point, its "ping"; a post about it selects the findings and links to it.

## Structure

1. **Abstract**, four sentences: the problem, why it matters, the idea, the result.
2. **Introduction**, one page: what the problem is, why it matters, why it is hard, why it is unsolved, then the contributions as a bulleted list of claims a reader could refute, each pointing to the section with its evidence.
3. **The problem**, with a running example before the general case.
4. **The idea**, intuition before detail.
5. **The details**: method, implementation.
6. **Evaluation**: absolute numbers, against a naive baseline, prior work and our own alternatives.
7. **Related work**, late, generous to others.
8. **Limits**, stated plainly.
9. **Conclusion**, half a page, concrete, not a copy of the abstract.

Tell the story of the result, not of how it was found.

## Title

Descriptive, or a memorable name and what it is.

## Opening

A concrete example of the problem.

## Evidence

Each contribution is refutable and points to its evidence. Every setup is described well enough to repeat. The summary matches the results exactly.

## Companion post

When a paper gets a blog post: findings first, figures annotated, the paper linked two or three times, a short limits paragraph, acknowledgements.

## Checklist

- [ ] One point, stated in the abstract.
- [ ] Contributions are refutable and point to their sections.
- [ ] A clear contribution appears before page three.
- [ ] An example comes before every general case.
- [ ] Evaluation against naive, prior and alternative baselines, with absolute numbers.
- [ ] Related work is late and generous.
- [ ] Limits are stated.
- [ ] Two first-time readers understood it, and their confusions were fixed in the text.

Drawn from: Simon Peyton Jones "How to write a great research paper", Jennifer Widom's tips, Distill, Anthropic and DeepMind research posts (see `registry.json`).
